What is on screen today

For comparison. The waterfall is trapped inside the receive pane, the panes are unequal, and the overlay tools take a fixed column down the right of the transmit side.

A1 — tools in a band below the canvas

What the parked work-in-progress did. The transmit pane becomes canvas-on-top, controls-underneath, exactly mirroring the receive pane. The properties for a selected item sit in one horizontal row rather than a stacked column.

Costs. Two rows of controls under the transmit canvas and one under the receive canvas means the panes are only equal in picture, not in total height — so the transmit side sets the window's floor. The property row has to fit five fields across a half-window; on a narrow window they wrap to a third row and the asymmetry grows.

A2 — properties become a dock

A thin strip of tool buttons stays under the canvas. The property editor — text, align, size, rotation, colour — moves into a dock at the bottom of the window, beside the status log, using the same mechanism. It appears when an item is selected and is closed the rest of the time.

Costs. The property editor is a long way from the item you are editing — bottom of the window, while the item is mid-canvas. And a panel that appears on selection shifts the layout underneath you, which is the thing tabs were meant to stop doing. It is also the most code: a second dock, its visibility rules, and a tab group with the log.

A3 — tools float over the canvas

Tool buttons in a small group in the canvas corner; properties in a popover anchored near the selected item. Nothing below the canvas but the send bar, so the two panes are equal in picture and in total height.

Costs. It is the least native-looking of the three, and you asked for traditional Qt rather than something that reads as designed-for-its-own-sake. Floating controls also cover the picture they are editing, and a popover needs its own placement logic so it never lands off-screen or on top of the selection.

Where do the transmit overlay tools live?

The one thing that stalled option A. Everything else about the layout follows from it.

Are the two panes locked to equal width?

You said "same size, or much closer". A splitter can start equal and still be dragged.

Chosen: locked equal, against the recommendation above — recording that rather than quietly rewriting it. The case for locking is good: the original complaint was that transmit had ~2.5× the received picture's area, and a draggable handle is a thing that gets knocked out of place and stays there. The case for draggable was that an odd screen might want one side wider. Cheap to revisit either way, since both are the same container. One consequence worth knowing: locked means the splitter is replaced by an equal-stretch layout rather than having its handle disabled, so the panes also stop being collapsible.

In tabbed mode, where does the full-width waterfall go?

Tabs exist for narrow screens, where vertical space is also scarce.

What this costs, and the bug it collides with

Worth seeing before you choose, because it changes which option is cheap. Switching to tabs today grows the window and it never shrinks back — you sent screenshots of exactly that. Measured on the pane container:

It is 0.75 × width: a widget asking to be 4:3 through heightForWidth, which a splitter ignores and a tab widget passes straight to the window. The transmit half of it is fixed; the receive half is not yet.

This matters for the choice above because every option here makes the panes wider than they are today, so any remaining aspect-ratio constraint gets more expensive, not less. It also means A is probably cheaper than it looked when I parked it — the 1586 px window minimum that stalled it was very likely this same class of bug rather than anything about the layout.
